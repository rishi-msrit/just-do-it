Johari Window

What it is

Created by psychologists Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in 1955 (combining their names "Jo" and "Hari"), the Johari Window is a cognitive model used to improve self-awareness, personal growth, and team communication. It divides your personality traits and behaviors into a 2×2 grid based on two variables: whether the traits are known or unknown to you, and whether they are known or unknown to the people around you.

The Four Panes of the Window

  • Open Area (Known to Self & Others): The space of public interaction, shared facts, and open communication. The larger this pane is, the healthier and more productive your relationships are.
  • Blind Spot (Unknown to Self, Known to Others): What others observe about you that you are unaware of (e.g., you talk too fast when nervous, or you tend to interrupt people when excited). Inviting honest feedback is the only way to shrink this quadrant.
  • Hidden Area / Facade (Known to Self, Unknown to Others): Things you know about yourself but choose to keep secret from others (e.g., a fear of public speaking, or a desire to switch careers). Sharing these with trusted peers helps build trust and connection.
  • Unknown Area (Unknown to Self & Others): Latent talents, untried skills, or unconscious behaviors that have not yet been tested by experience. Stepping out of your comfort zone is how you discover what lies here.

How to Grow Self-Awareness

Self-improvement is defined by expanding your Open Area. You accomplish this in two ways:

1. Invite Feedback: Ask peers or mentors what they observe about you. This shifts items from your Blind Spot into the Open Area.

2. Practice Self-Disclosure: Share your fears, hopes, or background stories. This shifts items from the Hidden Area into the Open Area.

Real-world Example

Example (Software Team Lead):
• Open: Everyone knows you are an expert in architecture design and love coffee.
• Blind Spot: Mentors point out that you sound defensive when juniors challenge your code during pull reviews.
• Hidden: You feel immense imposter syndrome about managing budget sheets.
• Unknown: You have never managed an active crisis—discovering you are calm under fire when the production database fails.
